    Section 2.2 All, some, none

    Suppose you are ordering pizza, and you have the options of ordering any combination of toppings from two topping choices: jalapenos, pineapple. (Only two for now, for calculating convenience. Not to worry, we will have more toppings later!) You may get no toppings, any one topping, or all toppings. How many options do you have? (As in, how many different kinds of pizzas can you order?)

    Section 2.2.1 Method 1.

    You may write down all options and count the number. Let’s assign jalapenos the letter J, and pineapples the letter P. Then you can have:
    1. neither J nor P (no toppings)
    2. J
    3. P
    4. JP
    So we get four options! Our answer is 4.
    Just one example does not give us a pattern. Let’s look at another. Let us add another topping (told you more were coming!): pepperoni, and let’s assign it the letter R. How many different combinations can you have now? Remember, you can have no toppings, some toppings, or all toppings.
    Let’s lay them out, and count them manually:
    1. no toppings
    2. J
    3. P
    4. R
    5. JP
    6. JR
    7. PR
    8. JPR
    So, that’s eight toppings. Our answer is 8. A pattern seems to be emerging. It looks like the answer for \(n\) toppings is \(2^n\text{.}\) Let’s verify for \(n=1\text{,}\) that is, one topping. Math says \(2^1\text{,}\) so 2 options. And counting the options agrees. We can either have that topping or not.
    Thus, \(2^n\) seems to be correct. And the \(n=1\) case tells us why. For every topping, there can be two options: either that topping is included or it is not. Thus, two options for each of \(n\) toppings will give us \(2^n\) options. Thus confirmed!
    Let’s have some more fun with the concept.
    You are at the Indian store buying samosas. These are delicious fried savory pastries in the shape of a tetrahedron (roughly). These are stuffed with spiced potatoes and peas, but you may add cashews and/or paneer (like tofu) to it. How many different types of samosas can you have?
    Think about it. Then let’s discuss.
    Since potatoes and peas are part of the samosa, but you can add or not add two more items -cashews and/or paneer - that gives us four options. You may include cashwes or not (2 options) and you may include paneer or not (2 options) giving total 2 times 2 = 4 options.
    Here’s another interesting one.
    In a burrito, your teacher may include any combination of 4 items: rice, beans, tofu, veggies. But, you must include at least one item. That is, the burrito cannot be empty. How many combinations can you make? Watch out, this one could be tricky if you are not careful.

    Checkpoint 2.2.1.

    In a burrito, your teacher may include any combination of 4 items: rice, beans, tofu, and veggies. But, you must include at least one item. That is, the burrito cannot be empty. How many combinations can you make? Watch out, this one could be tricky if you are not careful.
    Hint.
    How many total combinations are possible with 4 items, including the empty set?
    Answer.
    15
    Solution.
    Each of the 4 items can either be included or not, so there are \( 2^4 = 16 \) total combinations. But one of these is the empty burrito (no items), which is not allowed. So there are \( 16 - 1 = 15 \) valid combinations.
    Now let us tackle the case where a few options are repeated. Take two words CAB and BIB.
    In how many different or distinct ways can you rearrange the letters of the word CAB? Let’s write them all out and count. CAB, CBA, ACB, ABC, BAC, BCA. Six distinct ways.
    Mathematically, how many ways to choose the first letter? 3. How many ways to choose the second letter? 2, since one of the letters is unavailable to be chosen again since it was already chosen as the first letter. And how many ways to pick the third letter? Only one is left, so one way to pick that one. Thus, total ways is 3 times 2 times 1 = 6. This, as we have seen before, is the factorial \(3!\)
    Now let us try the same thing with BIB. We get BIB, BBI, IBB. Only three distinct ways. This is because, since the two letters B are indistinguishable from each other, any exchange of those two letters does not give us distinct arrangements. So the answer here is 3, which is half of 6 for the other word CAB.
    Again, with just one example, we do not see the pattern. So let’s do another. Four letter words! Say, CORN and MOOO.
    How many arrangements with CORN? Four distinct letters, therefore \(4! = 24\text{.}\)
    For MOOO, however, there are fewer. Let’s count them all. MOOO, OMOO, OOMO, OOOM. Just four distinct arrangements. There are \(4! = 24\) possible, but 3 letters are identical, and those three letters can be arranged in \(3! = 6\) different ways. Thus you are dividing the total possible number by the total repeated, indistinct possibilities, which is \(3!\text{.}\) Thus the total distinct arrangements in this case is \(4!\) divided by \(3!\text{,}\) which is \(24/6 = 4\text{.}\)
    General rule. The number of distinct arrangements of \(n\) items of which \(m\) are repeated is \(n!/m!\) . If there are multiple repeated items, you divide by the factorial of all the ones that are repeated. For example, for BOOM, total distinct ways is \(4!/2! = 12\text{.}\) For MOMO, total distinct ways is \(4!/(2!*2!) = 6\text{.}\) This is since two letters are repeated twice, M and O.
